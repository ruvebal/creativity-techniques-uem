## Why this cycle

Most creativity techniques you use in this course — brainstorming first among them — were not born in art schools. They were born in American advertising, industrial laboratories and Cold War research policy. This cycle asks you to see that history before you use the tools.

The cycle question:

> How do collective acts of invention become products, private property, industrial systems — and stories about one genius?

We answer it on **three historical layers**. Keep them apart: each one changed what “creative” meant and who profited from it.

### Layer 1 · Industry organises invention (1870s–1920s)

Invention moved from the workshop into the company laboratory. After the Second World War, Osborn could take this for granted, writing that “most of the best ideas are now being produced by laboratory staffs through organized research,” describing a research centre where 250 workers in twelve specialised teams hunted for ideas every day [(Osborn 2013, chap. 25)](#ref-osborn-2013). The work is collective; the name on the product is usually one.

Mass manufacturing also redesigned *how long* products should live. In the 1920s manufacturers deliberately planned ways to make their products obsolete. Norman retells a story about Henry Ford buying scrapped Ford cars to find the parts that were still in good shape — so they could be redesigned to fail at the same time as the others and save money [(Norman 2013, 291–92)](#ref-norman-2013). Creativity, here, serves the balance sheet.

That laboratory-and-firm pattern is what later writers read as industrial capitalism’s default. Langlois, rereading Schumpeter and Chandler, notes that both treated the large corporation as the “end-point of industrial capitalism,” then records the late-twentieth-century “unmaking of the corporate revolution” [(Langlois 2007, 9)](#ref-langlois-2007). Chandler’s own distinction, quoted there, is the one this cycle uses: the managerial firm is run by salaried professionals and comprises multiple units, each of which could have stood alone — the market still generates demand, but the firm takes over coordinating flows of goods, funds and people [(Langlois 2007, 10)](#ref-langlois-2007). In nineteenth-century America that takeover was not a smooth upgrade. Taking advantage of mass markets required what Langlois calls a systemic innovation: the “creative destruction of existing decentralized systems of production and distribution” in steel, farm machinery, cigarettes and branded goods [(Langlois 2007, 17)](#ref-langlois-2007). Unexpected fairs and unexpected products sit on that wreckage.

**Lesson 1 (Edison)** works on this layer: the laboratory, the patents, the investors and the public story.

### Layer 2 · The Cold War makes creativity a national resource (1940s–1960s)

Alex Osborn, an advertising executive in Buffalo, New York, formalised brainstorming in 1941 as a disciplined group practice [(Michalko 2010, 366)](#ref-michalko-2010). In *Applied Imagination* he framed idea production as national strategy: “Why not seek suggestions from amateurs for our cold war just as we sought them for our last World War?” — recalling that the National Inventors’ Council had collected 200,000 ideas between 1942 and 1945 [(Osborn 2013, chap. 1)](#ref-osborn-2013).

The state followed. The Soviet launch of Sputnik 1 in 1957 led to the creation of NASA and DARPA and to more US government spending on scientific research and education [(Chen 2011, 1)](#ref-chen-2011). A defence review, Project Hindsight, concluded that science and technology funds managed for defence purposes had been about an order of magnitude more efficient at producing useful events than funds invested without defence concerns [(Chen 2011, 73)](#ref-chen-2011). The research programmes outlived the period: by the 1990s Csikszentmihalyi could point to a laboratory studying creativity in children and a centre applying creativity knowledge to businesses [(Csikszentmihalyi 1996, notes to chap. 1)](#ref-csikszentmihalyi-1996).

**Lesson 2 (Fuller)** works partly on this layer. Brookes, Mould and Squire note that many of Fuller’s ideas gained prominence during the Cold War — nuclear threat, anti-communism, overpopulation and housing shortage sitting next to rapid military and consumer innovation — and that the same designs were used to promote American efficiency and commercial supremacy while Fuller became a counterculture hero [(Brookes, Mould, and Squire 2026, 2)](#ref-brookes-mould-squire-2026). In the 1950s the US military used his domes for troop housing, hardware shelters and the Distant Early Warning radar line across Canada, Greenland and Alaska [(Brookes, Mould, and Squire 2026, 4)](#ref-brookes-mould-squire-2026).

### Layer 3 · Capital adopts creativity as a brand (late 20th century – today)

This layer is *after* the Cold War and must not be confused with its origins. Design thinking reached Davos, TED and the *Harvard Business Review* [(Kimbell 2011, 287)](#ref-kimbell-2011). Kimbell links that success to a “new spirit” of capitalism that absorbed its critics and recast work as teams and networks — and notes that the 2008 crisis left high finance with unanswered questions about governance, accountability and values [(Kimbell 2011, 288)](#ref-kimbell-2011).

Public money still sits under private products. Mejias and Couldry report that Apple relied on research by government-funded scientists for the technical innovations behind the iPod, iPhone and iPad, and that DARPA funding started the internet [(Mejias and Couldry 2024, chap. 4)](#ref-mejias-couldry-2024). Both lessons end on this layer with a **software transfer**: who made it, who owns it, who controls access.

## Five connections to test

These are claims to *test* on every case, not conclusions. The table names each connection; the prose below says how to use it.

| Connection | Mechanism to look for | Tested in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Capital → creativity** | Who pays decides what gets invented and kept | Edison investors · Cold War funding · software |
| **Industry → creativity** | Team labour, one public name; a manufacturer who never becomes the public inventor | Edison laboratory · Beech Aircraft / Wichita House |
| **Greed → design** | Products shaped for profit, not use | Planned obsolescence · market control |
| **Holdings → unfairness** | Patents, pools and companies concentrate control; inventorship is assigned more sparingly than authorship | Edison cases · team credit (Lissoni) · Fuller patents vs corporations |
| **The unexpected** | Breakthroughs from public money, amateurs, collectives | Wartime idea drives · DARPA · Fuller’s systems |

**How to use it.** For any product, write one sentence per row with evidence: a document, a contract, a patent, a price, a credit line. If you cannot find evidence, write “no evidence yet” — that is an honest result, not a failure.

**Greed is a hypothesis, not a verdict.** Profit-seeking can fund real invention *and* distort it. Ask what the money made possible, and what it closed off.

**The unexpected rarely comes from where the story says.** The cases above show breakthroughs drawing on amateurs’ ideas, state programmes and team labour. Ask who is left out of the launch story.

**Credit is negotiated, then sold as genius.** Authorship and inventorship are both “attribution rights” — signals used by funders and firms — but they are not the same right, and teams now produce most papers and patents [(Lissoni, Montobbio, and Zirulia 2013, 50)](#ref-lissoni-montobbio-zirulia-2013). In matched patent–publication pairs, first and last authors are less likely to be left off the patent; junior and female co-authors are more likely to be excluded [(Lissoni, Montobbio, and Zirulia 2013, 67)](#ref-lissoni-montobbio-zirulia-2013). The legal figure of the inventor still dates from a nineteenth-century defence of patents that portrayed inventors as “heroes of the industrial revolution”; scientific credit is still modelled on an “individual spark of genius” even though the work is done in teams [(Lissoni, Montobbio, and Zirulia 2013, 67)](#ref-lissoni-montobbio-zirulia-2013). That is the Edison problem in one paragraph: the story the market needs is not the labour the product required. Fuller, writing as a patentee, says the same from the other side: most extant inventions already belong to corporations and governments; “corporations are legal fabrications; they cannot invent and discover”; he took patents first for credit and cost recovery, “but most importantly … to avoid being stopped by others — in particular, corporations and governments” [(Fuller 1975, 250.41)](#ref-fuller-1975-synergetics).

## Fairs and fairness

Industrial creativity is shown to the public at **fairs**: world’s fairs, national exhibitions, trade shows. The cycle’s working hypothesis: a fair turns collective, financed work into a national or corporate achievement — and that is where credit gets assigned. The cycle asks a second question about **fairness**: who is credited, who is paid, who keeps control?

Leads for investigation:

- How electric lighting was displayed at the great American exhibitions of the 1880s–1890s, and which companies won the contracts.
- Where and why Fuller’s geodesic domes were used at international exhibitions in the 1950s–1960s, and who paid for them. Military use on the Distant Early Warning line is now referenced [(Brookes, Mould, and Squire 2026, 4)](#ref-brookes-mould-squire-2026); Expo 67 is still to verify.
- How consumer goods were used as Cold War propaganda at exhibitions abroad.

Bring evidence for one lead to the bridge dossier between the two sessions.

## The two lessons

### 1 · Who Designs a Product—and Who Owns Its Story? (Edison)

Five Edison cases, each a different kind of attribution problem — improvement, purchase, collaboration, litigation, market control. You separate four questions that the genius story merges: **who made it, who owns it, whose name is sold, who controls access**. Output: an authorship map and a value-flow map.

### 2 · Can We Redesign the Whole System? (Fuller)

The same questions, applied to a designer who tried to change systems, not single products. Fuller is a **comparison**, not Edison’s moral opposite.

He opens *Operating Manual for Spaceship Earth* with a shipwreck: a floating piano lid may save you, but that is not how to design a life preserver, and we keep using yesterday’s accidents as if they were the only solutions [(Fuller 1969, 2)](#ref-fuller-1969). Treating specialization as the key to success, he writes, blocks comprehensive thinking, so industry’s gains are realized “only in negative ways, in new weaponry or the industrial support only of war faring” [(Fuller 1969, 3)](#ref-fuller-1969). *Synergetics* names the counter-move: synergy is “behavior of whole systems unpredicted by the behavior of their parts taken separately” [(Fuller 1975, 101.01)](#ref-fuller-1975-synergetics). Ephemeralization is the Dymaxion principle of doing ever more with ever less weight, time and energy, so that more people are served at higher standards with the same materials [(Fuller 1979, 175)](#ref-fuller-1979-synergetics-2).

The dwellings show who would have had to manufacture that system. Fuller researched obsolescence in architecture; the Dymaxion House and the Wichita House are studies in prefabrication, lightness and industrialization [(Serra Soriano, Merí de la Maza, and Díaz Segura 2025, 133)](#ref-serra-soriano-meri-diaz-2025). The Wichita House is named for Beech Aircraft, the company that manufactured it [(Serra Soriano, Merí de la Maza, and Díaz Segura 2025, 145)](#ref-serra-soriano-meri-diaz-2025). Conventional houses are hard to mass-produce; the Dymaxion Dwelling Machine was designed as a mass-production house [(Lee 2023, 1)](#ref-lee-2023). A prototype was shown in October 1945; Fuller’s insistence on perfecting it, and the high costs of mass production, slowed industrialization [(Serra Soriano, Merí de la Maza, and Díaz Segura 2025, 147)](#ref-serra-soriano-meri-diaz-2025). Only one prototype of the Dwelling Machine was produced [(Lee 2023, 7)](#ref-lee-2023). Ask: who would manufacture, finance, install and maintain the design — and who owns the story when the factory never scales?

The lesson closes with the software transfer.
