This is a **Master Lecture** — a theory session shared across Creativity Techniques. It is **not** one of the official course units. It gives you one map of creative change (Margaret Boden's *conceptual spaces*) and two early critiques of that map, so you can name *how* a design idea is new.

**Rhythm:** Analysis → Masterclass (six ideas + recall check) → Lab (two exercises, portfolio-bound).

</div>

## Learning objectives

1. Define a creative idea as **new, surprising and valuable**, and tell **P-creativity** (new to you) from **H-creativity** (new to history).
2. Describe a design style as a **conceptual space** with rules, and sort an idea by the road it took: **combine**, **explore** or **transform**.
3. Change one rule of a style on purpose — negate it, drop it, or ask what it is for — and explain what the change opened up.
4. Explain two critiques of the conceptual-spaces map (**creative systems and search problems**; **ordinary mechanisms and process**) and use them to judge your own studio process.

## Analysis

Before any theory, look at one piece of design work — a garment, a poster, a chair, an app screen — and ask three questions you already know from the course: **language**, **medium** and **support**.

**Language** is the style the piece speaks: its grammar of shapes, colours, proportions and references. Today we call that grammar a *conceptual space* and try to write its rules down. **Medium** is the material and technique — knit or print, pixels or plywood. A medium is also a set of rules: it makes some moves easy and others nearly impossible. **Support** is where the work lives and who meets it — a shop window, a feed, a gallery, a jury. Support decides who gets to say whether the piece is *valuable*, and many arguments about creativity are really arguments about value [(Boden 2004, 10)](#ref-boden-2004).

Then ask the lecture's own question: **which road did this piece take?** Did it **combine** familiar ideas in a new way, **explore** what its style could already do, or **transform** the style by changing one of its rules? Name the rule. If you cannot name the rule, you cannot yet say it was broken.

When D1 defences are running (two per session, up to 15 minutes each), use this lens for one question to the presenter: *which rule of the style held, and which one changed?* See [D1 · Live creative-process analysis]({{ '/assignments/en/creative-process-analysis-presentation/' | relative_url }}).

## Masterclass — six ideas

### 1 · New, surprising, valuable — new to whom?

Boden's working definition is short:

> “Creativity is the ability to come up with ideas or artefacts that are new, surprising and valuable.”
>
> <p class="quote-ref" markdown="1">— (<a href="#ref-boden-2004">Boden 2004, 1</a>)</p>

She adds that creativity is not a rare faculty: “every one of us is creative, to a degree” [(Boden 2004, 1)](#ref-boden-2004). The key move is to split *new* in two. An idea is **P-creative** (psychological) when it is new to the person who has it, however many people had it before; it is **H-creative** (historical) when, as far as we know, nobody had it before [(Boden 2004, 2)](#ref-boden-2004). For a student, P-creativity is the one that matters: your idea counts even if a famous designer had it first. Instead of “creative, yes or no?”, ask “Just how creative is it, and in just which way(s)?” [(Boden 2004, 2)](#ref-boden-2004).

### 2 · A conceptual space is a style you can map

> “Conceptual spaces are structured styles of thought.”
>
> <p class="quote-ref" markdown="1">— (<a href="#ref-boden-2004">Boden 2004, 4</a>)</p>

Boden's examples include ways of writing, styles of painting or music, scientific theories — and “fashions in couture or choreography” [(Boden 2004, 4)](#ref-boden-2004). You usually pick a space up from your culture or peer group; no single person invents it [(Boden 2004, 4)](#ref-boden-2004). Later in the book she calls these spaces *maps of the mind*: rule systems that guide thought and action into some paths but not others [(Boden 2004, 59)](#ref-boden-2004). In studio terms, a space is the set of rules you could write on one card — “oversized shoulders, raw hems, black only” — that let you recognise a piece as belonging to a style.

### 3 · Three roads: combine, explore, transform

Boden names three ways creativity happens, matched to three kinds of surprise [(Boden 2004, 3)](#ref-boden-2004).

- **Combine.** “The first involves making unfamiliar combinations of familiar ideas” [(Boden 2004, 3)](#ref-boden-2004) — collage, textile art, analogy. The combination only works if there is a link people can follow; random pairing is not enough [(Boden 2004, 3)](#ref-boden-2004).
- **Explore.** Work inside a space and find what it can already do. Exploration helps you see possibilities you had not glimpsed before [(Boden 2004, 4)](#ref-boden-2004).
- **Transform.** Change the space itself, so that ideas become possible that were unthinkable before. “The supposedly impossible idea can come about only if the creator changes the pre-existing style in some way” [(Boden 2004, 6)](#ref-boden-2004).

The maps in our heads, and the ones our communities favour, can change — “and it’s creative thinking which changes them” [(Boden 2004, 5)](#ref-boden-2004).

### 4 · Rules are the material of change

It is tempting to think creativity means having no rules. Boden says the opposite: “far from being the antithesis of creativity, constraints on thinking are what make it possible” [(Boden 2004, 95)](#ref-boden-2004).

So how do you change a rule? One general move is to **consider the negative** — negate one aspect of the problem; applied to a structural rule, not a surface detail, this can redraw the whole space at once [(Boden 2004, 66)](#ref-boden-2004). Her music example is Schoenberg: by dropping even the rule that a piece comes to rest on its home chord, he stepped out of tonality into a new field governed by different rules [(Boden 2004, 72)](#ref-boden-2004). Reviewing the book, Perkins lists more of these moves, including focusing on function — “asking not how is this usually done but what is this supposed to do” [(Perkins 1995, 103)](#ref-perkins-1995).

There is a catch that matters for students: “To recognize a structural novelty, one needs a structured mind” [(Boden 2004, 74)](#ref-boden-2004). You can only change a rule on purpose if you know the rule.

### 5 · The critics: search, systems and process

Two reviews from 1995 push back on the map. (Both review the first edition of the book; we read the second.)

**Perkins: look at creative systems, not only minds.** He defines creative systems as systems “that over time yield adaptive novelty, that is, unexpected outcomes that are nonetheless functional in context” [(Perkins 1995, 98)](#ref-perkins-1995) — a mind, a team, evolution, a culture. He treats creating as search through a space of possibilities and names four ways search gets stuck: solutions are **rare**; they are **isolated** behind the rules; search lingers on an **oasis** “close to success but not quite there”; or it wanders a flat **plateau** with no sign of where to go [(Perkins 1995, 99–100)](#ref-perkins-1995). Only isolation asks you to extend the space; the oasis and the plateau trap a search inside a space it already has [(Perkins 1995, 106–7)](#ref-perkins-1995). His challenge: Boden's account “allows for but perhaps underplays” creative outcomes from systems that never change their own rules [(Perkins 1995, 105)](#ref-perkins-1995). His summary line is worth keeping:

> “Problems are hard creatively when the circumstances mask novel solutions.”
>
> <p class="quote-ref" markdown="1">— (<a href="#ref-perkins-1995">Perkins 1995, 108</a>)</p>

**Ram and colleagues: show the process, not just the possibility.** They praise the book, but say it “leaves most details of the processes of creativity and their interactions unexplicated” [(Ram et al. 1995, 111)](#ref-ram-1995) — while giving “a strong argument that such an explanation is possible” [(Ram et al. 1995, 111)](#ref-ram-1995). They also doubt that people who produce many good ideas go through radical conceptual change for each one [(Ram et al. 1995, 114)](#ref-ram-1995).

### 6 · Ordinary minds, practised — and value is argued

Ram and colleagues argue that “extraordinary outcomes arise from the application of ordinary mechanisms, enhanced and applied with conscious (strategic) control” [(Ram et al. 1995, 112)](#ref-ram-1995). Creativity is not a switch you turn on or off; it comes from how inferences, knowledge, the task, the situation and your control of the process work together [(Ram et al. 1995, 116)](#ref-ram-1995). Their design example is a student team building a device to move eggs: remembering how a submarine launches missiles one at a time led them to redescribe the problem as launching each egg separately [(Ram et al. 1995, 116)](#ref-ram-1995). “Innovation often arises when ideas from one culture are applied in another” [(Ram et al. 1995, 117)](#ref-ram-1995) — one design culture lending to another. And practice changes you: as you gain knowledge, strategies and methods, the space you search, and how you search it, changes too [(Ram et al. 1995, 126)](#ref-ram-1995).

Value stays the hard part. Boden notes that values change, differ across cultures and are argued over inside them — think of flared trousers — and that the shock of a transformed style can hide its value even from fellow artists [(Boden 2004, 10)](#ref-boden-2004). The same problem returns with machines: programs that evolve images can make transformations easily; what is difficult is stating our aesthetic values clearly enough for a program to judge them, so a person still picks what survives [(Boden 2004, 9)](#ref-boden-2004). For this course that is an authorship question: whoever chooses holds part of the authorship.

## Lab (Portfolio)

*Two exercises come next. Everything you produce in Lab goes into your **portfolio index**. Exercise 1 maps and explores a style; Exercise 2 changes one of its rules.*

### Exercise 1 — Map a style, then explore it {#lab-exercise-1}

Practises Masterclass ideas **2** (a style is a space you can map) and **3** (combine, explore, transform).

**Time:** 25 minutes. **Group:** pairs (solo works). **Materials:** paper or a table template, pens; one style chosen by the pair.

**Steps:**

1. Pick a design style you know well (for example: a streetwear sneaker, a gig poster, a café menu, a minimalist phone case).
2. Write **six rules** of that style, one per row: shape, material, colour, type or pattern, user, place.
3. Sketch **three quick variants** that keep **every** rule. This is exploration: what can the style already do that you had not tried?
4. Sketch **one variant** that joins the style to an idea from a distant field (sport, cooking, architecture, nature). Write the **link** in one line — a combination needs a reason people can follow.
5. Under each sketch, write which road it took: *explore* or *combine*. Keep the rule list for Exercise 2.

**Portfolio trace:** the six-rule list; four thumbnails; one road label per thumbnail.

**Source:** steps are a classroom adaptation of attribute listing. The theory behind them: conceptual spaces as styles [(Boden 2004, 4)](#ref-boden-2004) and the three roads [(Boden 2004, 3)](#ref-boden-2004).

### Exercise 2 — Change one rule {#lab-exercise-2}

Practises Masterclass ideas **4** (rules are the material of change) and **5** (search problems).

**Time:** 25 minutes. **Group:** the same pairs. **Materials:** the rule list and sketches from Exercise 1.

**Steps:**

1. Circle the rule from Exercise 1 that feels most fixed — the one nobody in that style would question.
2. Change it with **one** move: **negate** it (do the opposite), **drop** it, or ask **what it is for** and meet that purpose another way.
3. Sketch one variant under the changed rule.
4. Add **one new rule** to replace the one you broke, so the new space still has a shape (constraints make creativity possible — see idea 4).
5. Swap with another pair. They answer three questions on your sheet: Is it new? Is it surprising? Is it valuable — and to whom?
6. Write one sentence: before the change, were you stuck on an *oasis* (almost good) or a *plateau* (no direction)?

**Portfolio trace:** the circled rule; the move you used; the new sketch; the replacement rule; the other pair's three answers; your oasis-or-plateau sentence.

**Source:** steps are a classroom adaptation of the reversal method. The moves come from Boden's heuristics [(Boden 2004, 66)](#ref-boden-2004) and Perkins's summary of them [(Perkins 1995, 103)](#ref-perkins-1995); the oasis and plateau come from Perkins [(Perkins 1995, 100)](#ref-perkins-1995). For a longer version of step 4, use the exercise datasheet [Imposing Constraints]({{ '/exercises/en/imposing-constraints/' | relative_url }}). For the negate-or-drop move as a full exercise, use [Consider the Negative]({{ '/exercises/en/consider-the-negative/' | relative_url }}).

## Carry forward

Write one sentence in your portfolio index for your current project: **one rule of its space you will keep, and one rule you will test.** Bring it to the next session — later sessions add other lenses on creativity (how juries judge it, how it grows over a career) and test this map against them.

## Tao of Creativity

- *A rule, not a cage — / an anchor you can lift once / when you know the sea.*
- **Student:** Does my idea count if history had it first? **Master:** The moon is not less full when seen through leaves — but do you see the gaps where the light passes?
- “Great leaps do not come from extraordinary thinking. They come from ordinary thinking used extraordinarily well.”
