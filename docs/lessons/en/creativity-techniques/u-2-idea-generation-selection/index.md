## 🎯 Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, you should be able to:

1. **Separate** generation and selection in time — schedule both, never conflate them.
2. **Distinguish** fluency (many ideas) from flexibility (many *kinds* of ideas) in your own work.
3. **Recognise** the first obvious answers and keep generating past them before you judge.
4. **Choose and defend** a selection criterion (novelty × fitness-to-brief) explicitly, in writing.
5. **Use** a role/constraint tool (Hats, Oblique Strategies, or equivalent) as a session structure — not a magic trick.

## Analysis

*Session block 1 · Analysis opens the class.* From session 3, two students × ~15 minutes each defend their D1 piece. Before you watch the deck, revisit the U1 method: **language / medium / support** first, **circulation** second. This unit adds one axis to that method — before you judge whether a piece is creative, ask *how many other pieces the maker considered and how they chose this one*.

## Masterclass

*Session block 2 · six ideas. Each one gives a claim, the evidence for it, a design example, and the Lab exercise where you try it. Read them as six moves you can try, not as six laws.*

### 1 · Generate, then select

**Generation and selection are two different jobs; doing them in the same minute makes both weaker.**

> The first stage is to make the map. The second stage is to choose a route on the map.
{: .unit-quote}
<p class="unit-quote-citation"><a href="#ref-debono-1985">(de Bono 1985, 199)</a></p>

Craft notes that one creative act may hold both divergent and convergent thinking [(Craft 2000, 30)](#ref-craft-2000); this course schedules the two moves apart. Judging too early has a cost: in a classic experiment, students who expected their artwork to be evaluated "produced artworks significantly lower on judged creativity" than students who did not [(Amabile 1979, 221)](#ref-amabile-1979). One small laboratory study points the same way: after "open-monitoring" meditation (noticing whatever comes, without fixing on one thing), people did better on a divergent-thinking task, while "focused-attention" meditation did not help a convergent task [(Colzato, Ozturk, and Hommel 2012, 1)](#ref-colzato-2012). Some briefs really do need the first answer: when time is the constraint, "generate, then select" is a luxury.

**In practice:** for a festival poster, you might fill a page with rough directions for ten minutes without crossing any out, and only then circle the three worth developing.

**Try it:** [Lab · Exercise 1](#lab-exercise-1) generates; [Lab · Exercise 2](#lab-exercise-2) selects from what Exercise 1 produced.

### 2 · Fluency with flexibility

**Count two things: how many ideas you have (fluency) and how many kinds of idea (flexibility).**

Deciding whether two ideas are different possibilities or variations of one is already a judgement, so whoever counts is judging more than they think. de Bono draws the same cut as a *method* distinction — "Lateral thinking is generative. Vertical thinking is selective" [(de Bono 1970, introduction)](#ref-debono-1970) — which is why selection should follow the period of production rather than interrupt it. Csikszentmihalyi also separates raw fluency from originality, the picking of unusual associations [(Csikszentmihalyi 1996, chap. 3)](#ref-csikszentmihalyi-1996); those unusual associations become material for later judgement.

**In practice:** for a packaging brief, twenty bottle shapes are high fluency but one kind; adding a refill pouch, a label-free bottle and a deposit scheme adds kinds.

**Try it:** in [Lab · Exercise 1](#lab-exercise-1), step 6 asks your team to count both numbers.

### 3 · Get past the obvious

**The first ideas that come to mind are often the obvious ones, and they stick.**

Studies of designers find that they tend to "hang on to their principal solution concept" even when problems appear, and that showing people an example solution makes their own designs copy its features — a "fixation" effect suggested by Jansson and Smith, as Cross notes [(Cross 2006, 81–82)](#ref-cross-2006). Norman gives designers the same advice: "Generate numerous ideas. It is dangerous to become fixated upon one or two ideas too early in the process" [(Norman 2013, 226)](#ref-norman-2013). Osborn's first ground rule for an idea session points the same way: "Criticism must be withheld until all ideas are in" [(Osborn 1942, chap. 4)](#ref-osborn-1942). de Bono calls the habit looking for alternatives instead of accepting the most obvious approach [(de Bono 1970, chap. "The Generation of Alternatives")](#ref-debono-1970), and puts it in one image:

> You cannot dig a hole in a different place by digging the same hole deeper.
{: .unit-quote}
<p class="unit-quote-citation"><a href="#ref-debono-1970">(de Bono 1970, introduction)</a></p>

<figure class="lesson-figure" id="figure-masterclass-3">
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/deck-media/182376a162650045.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="A wall covered with columns of handwritten blue and orange sticky notes from a brainstorming session, each note a separate idea kept without judging." loading="lazy" decoding="async">
<figcaption>{% include lesson-figure.html deck="u-2-idea-generation-selection" slide="masterclass-3" %}</figcaption>
</figure>

**In practice:** for wayfinding signs, you might find that arrows and room numbers come first; if so, keep writing until colour, sound or floor marks appear.

**Try it:** the silent rounds of [Lab · Exercise 1](#lab-exercise-1) keep ideas coming without judging them.

### 4 · Role and constraint tools

**Role and constraint tools change who speaks and how, so the first voice in the room does not narrow everyone else.**

In a Six Thinking Hats round, everyone thinks in one mode at a time: white (facts), red (feelings), black (caution: why it might not work), yellow (benefits), green (new ideas), blue (running the process). An *Oblique Strategies* card is a deck of brief creative prompts — not a theory or a guarantee of a better idea.

> Any tool should be useful in the expected way, but a truly great tool lends itself to uses you never expected.
{: .unit-quote}
<p class="unit-quote-citation"><a href="#ref-raymond-2001">(Raymond 2001, 44)</a></p>

de Bono names a related move: use information to provoke a new pattern [(de Bono 1970, chap. "Difference between Lateral and Vertical Thinking")](#ref-debono-1970). “What if?” thinking makes people consider alternatives [(Wong, Galinsky, and Kray 2009, 161)](#ref-wong-2009), but in one line of studies it helped people link given ideas and hurt the generation of new ones [(Wong, Galinsky, and Kray 2009, 168)](#ref-wong-2009).

<figure class="lesson-figure" id="figure-masterclass-4">
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/deck-media/56194141c1329fd1.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="A workshop board headed 'Sombrero negro (Dudas)' — the black thinking hat — asking 'What are the doubts? What are the risks?', with coloured sticky-note answers below." loading="lazy" decoding="async">
<figcaption>{% include lesson-figure.html deck="u-2-idea-generation-selection" slide="masterclass-4" %}</figcaption>
</figure>

**In practice:** in an app-screen critique, give everyone three minutes of black hat only, so caution arrives from all sides at once.

**Try it:** [Lab · Exercise 2](#lab-exercise-2) ends with a yellow-hat round, then a black-hat round.

### 5 · Name selection criteria

**Name the criterion before you choose; unstated criteria favour whatever the room already leaned toward.**

The scoring is the design act, not the ranking. A practitioner line calls editing a demonstration of taste revealed in curation — what is included, what is not, how the pieces sit together [(Rubin 2023, 386)](#ref-rubin-2023): selection criteria by another name, not a substitute for writing them down. Persaud points out that creativity "is usually defined in terms of the production end of ideas or products," while the process by which ideas are "critically evaluated, selected, altered or dismissed" gets far less attention [(Persaud 2007, 68)](#ref-persaud-2007). Some briefs reward the obvious fit; the open question is whose criteria decide what counts as "novel".

<figure class="lesson-figure" id="figure-masterclass-5">
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/deck-media/d34fe8bf4ea9f618.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="A Pugh concept-selection spreadsheet: criteria such as reliability and safety with weights in rows, four design concepts scored in columns, and totals at the bottom." loading="lazy" decoding="async">
<figcaption>{% include lesson-figure.html deck="u-2-idea-generation-selection" slide="masterclass-5" %}</figcaption>
</figure>

**In practice:** before choosing a chair concept, write “sits 20 people in a small café, stacks in one corner” on the board, then compare every sketch against that line.

**Try it:** in [Lab · Exercise 2](#lab-exercise-2), write your criterion before you pick one idea.

### 6 · Workshops are means, not genius

**A workshop produces material you did not have before; that does not prove it produced better ideas.**

The dimensions Csikszentmihalyi lists in his chapter on the creative personality — fluency, flexibility, originality — are the ones most creativity tests measure and most workshops try to improve [(Csikszentmihalyi 1996, chap. 3)](#ref-csikszentmihalyi-1996). Workshops try to raise those scores; landmark work, by contrast, is rare, depends on its system, and cannot be produced on command in a two-hour block. A practitioner counterpoint insists that the only practice that matters is the one you keep doing, and that any method should be tested on yourself rather than copied [(Rubin 2023, 326)](#ref-rubin-2023).

<figure class="lesson-figure" id="figure-masterclass-6">
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/deck-media/c839dd8e96b50396.webp' | relative_url }}" alt="Van Gogh's 1889 self-portrait: the painter in a cap and green coat, a white bandage wrapped around his head, with a Japanese print on the wall behind him." loading="lazy" decoding="async">

**In practice:** after an exhibition-design workshop, your wall holds forty sticky notes; judge each against the exhibition’s brief before calling any of them better.

**Try it:** after [Lab · Exercise 2](#lab-exercise-2), ask which idea on your sheets could become evidence for D1, and what criterion would justify that choice.

## Lab (Portfolio)

*Session block 3 · A geometrical slide announces the Lab: two exercises follow, and everything you produce goes into your **portfolio index**. Exercise 1 generates; Exercise 2 selects from what Exercise 1 produced — the two jobs of this unit, in order.*

### Exercise 1 — 6-3-5 brainwriting against solo writers {#lab-exercise-1}

Practises Masterclass ideas **1** (generate, then select), **2** (fluency with flexibility) and **3** (get past the obvious).

**Time:** 25 minutes (an optional 3-minute warm-up, 3 rounds of 4 minutes, 8–10 minutes to pool, count and compare). **Shortened:** in a team of six, passing every sheet to everyone takes six rounds (about 30 minutes); this Lab runs **3 rounds**.

**Group:** the class splits into teams of six (3–6 works). Half the teams brainwrite. The other half are **solo teams**: the same number of people, each writing alone in silence; their lists are pooled only at the end.

**Materials:** one brief for the whole class; for each brainwriter, a sheet with three columns and three rows; pens; the slide timer.

**Steps:**

1. *(Optional warm-up, 3 minutes.)* Sit comfortably and notice whatever thoughts, sounds or sensations come, without holding on to any. Then start straight away.
2. **Brainwriting teams:** write three ideas for the brief in the first row of your sheet, one per column.
3. When the round ends, pass your sheet to the left. Read what is there, then write three new ideas in the next row — new, or built on the ones above. Nobody talks.
4. Run **3 rounds** in total.
5. **Solo teams:** each person writes ideas alone for the same 12 minutes. No sheets are passed.
6. Each team pools its ideas, removes repeats and counts two numbers: different ideas (fluency) and different kinds of idea (flexibility). Allow 8–10 minutes for this.
7. Put every team's two numbers on the board and compare. Which kind of team found more kinds of idea? Keep all sheets for Exercise 2.

**Facilitation note:** 6-3-5 caps each writer at three ideas per round; solo writing has no cap. Say this aloud, and compare kinds of idea (flexibility) and ideas built on someone else's, not raw counts.

**Portfolio trace:** your sheet or solo list; your team's two numbers; the class comparison; one sentence on what passing sheets (or working alone) did to your ideas.

**Example trace:** *Illustrative example (not student work)* — made by the professor to show the format.

<div class="lesson-example-trace" markdown="1">
- **Brief:** a reusable cup for the campus café.
- **My sheet, row 1 (mine):** cup with a lid that doubles as a coaster · cup that folds flat · cup sold with a refill stamp card.
- **Row 2 (built on row 1):** coaster lid printed with the café map · folding cup that clips to a bag · stamp card printed on the cup itself.
- **Row 3:** cup that changes colour with heat · cup lent and returned like a library book · map lid that shows the nearest water fountain.
- **Team numbers:** 41 different ideas · 7 kinds (material, shape, service, information, reward, lending, behaviour).
- **Class comparison:** copied from the board on the day (brainwriting and solo teams, ideas and kinds).
- **Note:** passing sheets pushed me into “service” ideas (lending, refills) that I did not write in row 1.
</div>

**Judged by:** [Portfolio rubric — creative fluency, flexibility, and originality]({{ '/assignments/en/creativity-techniques-portfolio/' | relative_url }}#rubric)

**Source:** 6-3-5 brainwriting and the solo-team comparison: classroom adaptation. The warm-up rests on one small laboratory study in which open-monitoring meditation helped a divergent-thinking task [(Colzato, Ozturk, and Hommel 2012, 1)](#ref-colzato-2012); that study used sessions of about 35 minutes, so our 3-minute version is untested.

**Opt-out:** the warm-up is optional. You may sit quietly, read the brief, or start writing early instead. Nobody gives instructions about posture or breathing.

### Exercise 2 — Select: hits, COCD box, then yellow and black hats {#lab-exercise-2}

Practises Masterclass ideas **4** (role and constraint tools) and **5** (name selection criteria).

**Time:** 20 minutes (about 5 for hits, 7 for the grid, 8 for the hats).

**Group:** the same teams as Exercise 1.

**Materials:** all idea sheets from Exercise 1 on a wall or table; small dot stickers (or marker dots); a two-by-two grid on the board or on paper; sticky notes.

**Steps:**

1. **Hits:** put all your team's ideas up. In silence, each person puts small dots on the ideas, or parts of ideas, that look interesting. No discussion yet.
2. Take the ideas with the most dots (around ten) to the grid.
3. **COCD box:** draw a two-by-two grid — how new the idea is (low / high) against how feasible it is (low / high). Place each idea after a short discussion.
4. Name the corners: **now** (feasible, ordinary), **wow** (feasible and original), **how** (original, not yet feasible); the fourth corner is dropped. Keep the wow ideas and choose a top three.
5. **Yellow hat** (3 minutes): everyone thinks only about why each of the top three could work.
6. **Black hat** (3 minutes): everyone thinks only about why each could fail or cannot be done.
7. Before you pick, write the criterion you will use; then choose one idea. Compare: is your pick also the idea your team placed as most original in the grid? If not, say why in one sentence.

**Optional peer rating (about 5 extra minutes):** before step 7, swap your top three with a neighbouring team (no names on the list). Each person rates the other team's three items alone and in silence on the printable [peer rating sheet]({{ '/practice/en/peer-rating-sheet/' | relative_url }}), then returns the sheet. Compare the averages you receive with your own grid.

**Portfolio trace:** a photo of the dots and the grid; the top three with their yellow and black notes; your criterion, the chosen idea and your one-sentence comparison.

**Example trace:** *Illustrative example (not student work)* — made by the professor to show the format, continuing the cup brief above.

<div class="lesson-example-trace" markdown="1">
- **Hits:** ten ideas with the most dots, among them the coaster lid with the café map, the lending cup and the colour-changing cup.
- **COCD box:** *now* — refill stamp card; *wow* — lending cup returned like a library book, map lid; *how* — colour-changing cup.
- **Top three:** lending cup · map lid · stamp card printed on the cup.
- **Yellow / black (lending cup):** yellow — no cup to remember, fewer paper cups; black — who washes the cups, and what if they are not returned?
- **Criterion (written before choosing):** can the café test it for two weeks without new machines?
- **Choice:** the stamp card printed on the cup. Our most original idea in the grid was the lending cup; we did not pick it because washing and returns fail our criterion.
</div>

**Judged by:** [Portfolio rubric — critical analysis and judgement]({{ '/assignments/en/creativity-techniques-portfolio/' | relative_url }}#rubric)

**Source:** silent dot stickers marking the interesting parts of each idea (a "heat map") come from a design-sprint handbook, where voting is a separate, later step [(Knapp, Zeratsky, and Kowitz 2016, chap. 10)](#ref-knapp-2016). In de Bono's Six Thinking Hats, the yellow hat "covers hope and positive thinking" and the black hat covers "the negative aspects - why it cannot be done" [(de Bono 1985, 32)](#ref-debono-1985). The COCD box, the order of the steps and the comparison question are a classroom adaptation.

## Conclusion

This unit trains a rhythm, not a definition. It does not settle where a new kind ends and a dressed-up repeat begins, how many ideas are enough for a given brief, or whether Hats and prompt cards improve every brief. Rubin’s practice-and-taste account sits beside Csikszentmihalyi’s test dimensions without becoming one doctrine, and Hüppauf and Wulf treat imagination, fantasy, and creativity as related terms that still need to be told apart [(Hüppauf and Wulf 2009, 21)](#ref-huppauf-wulf-2009). Brainwriting reliably produces material you did not have in view at the start; whether that material is better is for your criteria to decide. Run the two Lab exercises twice, compare your notes, and decide which gap you want to investigate next.

## Editorial note. Work in progress. Teaching Innovation Practice
{: .lesson-editorial-note }

Csikszentmihalyi (1996) supports the fluency/flexibility/originality triad reused from U1. de Bono (1970) supports the generative/selective cut, the habit of delaying the obvious approach, and the provocative use of information. de Bono (1985) is the source for the map-then-route description of the Six Thinking Hats method and for the yellow- and black-hat definitions used in the Lab. Knapp, Zeratsky and Kowitz (2016) support the silent dot step; 6-3-5 brainwriting and the COCD box run as classroom adaptations until their sources are verified. Rubin (2023) gives a practitioner stance on consistent practice and editing-as-taste; it does **not** validate classroom hour schemes or replace peer-reviewed pedagogy. Wong, Galinsky and Kray (2009) support counterfactual alternative-thinking as a psychology bridge, with its limits. Osborn (1942), Amabile (1979), Colzato and colleagues (2012), Cross (2006), Norman (2013) and Persaud (2007) support deferred judgement, the cost of expected evaluation, the attention exercise, fixation and the neglect of selection. Hüppauf and Wulf (2009) support the point that imagination, fantasy, and creativity are related but not interchangeable terms. Experimental evidence on group brainstorming, and on whether later ideas are more original than first ones, is not yet cited here; the lesson does not make those claims. Where Craft (2000, 30) supports doing divergent and convergent work in one creative act, it does not say when a brief should skip generation; that line is still open. The design examples and the example traces are written by the professor to illustrate the ideas; they are not research findings or student work. None of these pages proves that *this* unit’s two Lab exercises are the only valid path to trained selection.
