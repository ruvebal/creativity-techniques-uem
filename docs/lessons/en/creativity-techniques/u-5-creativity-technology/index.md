## 🎯 Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, you should be able to:

1. Place generative tools in an **early variation** stage — not as a substitute for final judgment.
2. **Document** prompts, edits, refusals, and selection decisions alongside outputs.
3. **Name** originality theatre when surfaces look creative but no problem is framed.
4. Argue what **assessment** should see under AI (collaboration and reflection), not only pixels.
5. **Maintain agency** — treat tools as collaborators or instruments, not authors of record.
6. **Situate** tool use beside at least one critical cost (infrastructure, labour, or environment) without pretending tools are neutral.

## Masterclass

*Session block 2 · six ideas. Each one gives a claim, a design example, and the Lab exercise where you try it.*

### 1 · Stage the tool

**Use generative tools for early variation; keep problem framing and final judgment human.**

Think of the studio loop you already know from U2–U3: open many directions, then close on purpose. Generative tools fit the **open** side when you need quick variants to react to — not the moment you sign the brief or submit the final export. Before you open a tool, say which stage you are in.

**In practice:** for a poster brief, you might generate three layout moods in ten minutes, then **choose one** and redraw the hierarchy by hand because the type sizes are wrong for your audience.

**Try it:** [Question-led rough prototype]({{ '/exercises/en/question-led-rough-prototype/' | relative_url }}) writes one test question before any build — tool or manual.

### 2 · Show the process

**Prompts, selection decisions, and re-authoring belong in the creative work — not only the polished output.**

A portfolio that shows only a final render teaches assessors to guess. A portfolio that shows **what you asked, what you kept, and what you refused** teaches them to read your judgment. Process transparency is an ethic you practice in files and captions, not a button in an app.

**In practice:** save a screenshot of the prompt, the three outputs you rejected, and the layer you redrew — in that order — next to the PDF you submit.

**Try it:** [Process trail]({{ '/exercises/en/process-trail/' | relative_url }}) orders every version and marks diverge/converge moves.

### 3 · Refuse originality theatre

**Fast, pretty images without problem framing are empty craft — they perform novelty without answering a brief.**

When everything looks “creative” at once, ask the boring question first: **what problem is this solving?** If there is no problem, the work may be decoration. Originality theatre is the gap between glossy surfaces and weak framing.

**In practice:** if a model returns four beautiful palettes but your brief is wayfinding for visitors with low vision, the theatre stops when you name contrast and distance — not when you pick the prettiest swatch.

**Try it:** [Question-led rough prototype]({{ '/exercises/en/question-led-rough-prototype/' | relative_url }}) forces a test question tied to the brief.

### 4 · Assess judgment, not only surfaces

**Studio assessment must see collaboration and reflection under AI — not only final pixels.**

Rubrics that reward only the last file train students to hide assistance and to confuse speed with skill. Assessment should be able to read **how** you collaborated with a tool: what you directed, what you fixed, what you rejected.

**In practice:** attach a half-page “judgment log” to a crit: three decisions only you made, with reasons.

**Try it:** [Process trail]({{ '/exercises/en/process-trail/' | relative_url }}) ends with a turning-point paragraph assessors can read.

### 5 · Agency stays with the designer

**Treat AI as collaborator or instrument — not as author of record.**

Tools can widen the search space; they do not sign contracts, face clients, or carry ethical responsibility for a misleading sign. Your name on the work means your judgment — including the choice **not** to use a tool.

**In practice:** when a model suggests copy for a public sign, you edit for clarity and liability; the model does not “co-author” the legal risk.

**Try it:** [Question-led rough prototype]({{ '/exercises/en/question-led-rough-prototype/' | relative_url }}) requires you to record human edits after any generated variant.

### 6 · Name the costs

**Critical reading of AI systems — power, labour, planet — belongs beside the toolkit.**

Design education should not treat tools as magic boxes. Energy, data labour, and infrastructure politics affect who benefits from “free” generation. Naming a cost does not forbid tool use; it **locates** your practice in a world others also inhabit.

**In practice:** one sentence in your process note: “This session used cloud generation; I do not know the energy source — here is what I still owe the brief.”

**Try it:** the [Workshop](#workshop) declaration asks for one open question about what your public piece still hides — including energy, data labour, or infrastructure you cannot yet name.

## Workshop {#workshop}

From session 4: Workshop time goes half to **D2 Transposition** and half to the **D3 Atrium** final event.

**Task:** draft a **one-page process declaration** for the piece you will show publicly. Include: the brief; which tool stages you used; what you refused; what you re-authored by hand; one open question for the audience.

**Done enough:** declaration saved in the portfolio index and linked from the piece you will circulate.

---

## Conclusion

This unit does not tell you never to use generative tools. It asks **when**, **for what**, and **with what trace** — so speed does not replace judgment. Which part of your next deliverable will still make sense if the tool disappears but the brief remains?
