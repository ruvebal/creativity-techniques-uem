Consider the Negative

List the unwritten rules of a familiar style or product type, then negate one deep rule — do its opposite — and sketch what becomes possible. Changing a structural rule, not a surface detail, can open a whole new space of designs.

  • pairs

Requirements

Time ~30 minutes
Group pairs
Access Rules can be listed in writing or spoken while a partner writes. Sketches, collages, or short written scenarios are all valid outputs. No movement required.

Materials

  • one familiar style or product type (for example a bus stop, a wedding invitation, a login screen)
  • paper or whiteboard
  • timer

Before you start

  • Choose something both partners know well enough to list its rules.
  • Negate a structural rule (how it works or is organised), not a surface detail like colour.
  • Keep the rule list; it is part of the trace.

Steps

Follow in order — your professor may shorten the clock.

  1. Together, list six rules that every example of the style follows, even if nobody says them out loud.
  2. Mark each rule as surface (looks) or structure (how it works). Circle the most structural rule.
  3. Negate it — write its opposite as a new rule (for example "a bus stop is where you wait" becomes "a bus stop is where you never wait").
  4. In ten minutes, sketch two or three designs that obey the negated rule and keep the other five.
  5. For comparison, try dropping the rule entirely instead of negating it. Note how the results differ.

Source

One very general heuristic is consider the negative. In other words, one way of getting a new slant on a problem is to negate some aspect of it, whether consciously or unconsciously. When this heuristic is applied to a structural aspect of the problem, as opposed to a mere superficial detail, it can change the conceptual geography in one step. (Negating a constraint is not the same as dropping it: wanting any nonred sweet is not the same as wanting any sweet, whatever its colour. However, dropping constraints is a general heuristic, too. We have already seen that Kekulé's visualization of his problem could have been transformed by dropping the 'strings-only' constraint. Likewise, dropping Euclid's sixth axiom - that parallel lines meet at infinity - led to a fundamentally different, non-Euclidean, geometry.)

— (Boden 2004, 66)

Classroom note. Boden describes "consider the negative" and "dropping a constraint" as general heuristics for transforming a conceptual space; she gives no classroom procedure. The six-rule list, the surface/structure sort, the timings, and the negate-versus-drop comparison are professor adaptations of the reversal method.

Portfolio

Six-rule list with the circled rule; the negated rule; two or three sketches; the drop comparison; one sentence.
