Morning Pages

Write three pages of fast, unedited longhand before you start a design task. The pages are private and never assessed; their job is to empty out the inner critic so the next idea pass starts with a clearer head.

  • ~20 min
  • individual
  • technique creative-journal
  • unit U1
  • unit U6

Source book Cameron, Julia. 2002. The Artist’s Way: A Spiritual Path to Higher Creativity. 10th anniversary ed. New York: Jeremy P. Tarcher/Putnam.

Requirements

Time ~20 minutes
Group individual
Access Students who cannot write longhand may type with the screen dimmed, dictate into a private voice memo, or write fewer, larger pages; the rule is continuous output without rereading. Any language is fine. Students may opt out of the in-class pass and do it at home instead.

Materials

  • three sheets of lined or plain paper (or a notebook)
  • pen or pencil
  • timer
  • an envelope or folder to keep the pages private

Before you start

  • Nobody reads the pages — not the professor, not a partner. Only the date and page count are shared.
  • Write by hand if you can; speed matters more than neatness.
  • Run it before a generation exercise in the same session, so the effect can be noticed.

Steps

Follow in order — your professor may shorten the clock.

  1. Set a timer for fifteen minutes and write the date at the top of page one.
  2. Write without stopping until you fill three pages. If nothing comes, write "nothing comes" until something does.
  3. When the critic starts commenting ("this is boring", "you can't spell"), write the comment down and keep going.
  4. Do not reread. Fold the pages and put them away.
  5. Start the next exercise straight away. Afterwards, note in one line whether the first ideas came faster or slower than usual.

Source

The morning pages are the primary tool of creative recovery. As blocked artists, we tend to criticize ourselves mercilessly. Even if we look like functioning artists to the world, we feel we never do enough and what we do isn't right. We are victims of our own internalized perfectionist, a nasty internal and eternal critic, the Censor, who resides in our (left) brain and keeps up a constant stream of subversive remarks that are often disguised as the truth. […] Make this a rule: always remember that your Censor's negative opinions are not the truth. This takes practice. By spilling out of bed and straight onto the page every morning, you learn to evade the Censor. Because there is no wrong way to write the morning pages, the Censor's opinion doesn't count. Let your Censor rattle on. (And it will.) Just keep your hand moving across the page.

— (Cameron 2002, “The Basic Tools”)

Classroom note. Cameron's tool is a daily, at-home practice done first thing in the morning; this classroom version is one fifteen-minute pass at the start of a studio session, done as a warm-up before idea generation. Repeating it at home for a week is optional homework. Timing, the opt-out, and the one-line note are professor adaptations. The three-pages, longhand, do-not-reread rules follow Cameron's workbook instructions; the quoted warrant is from The Artist's Way.

Portfolio

Date and page count only (the pages stay private); one line on how the next idea pass felt.
