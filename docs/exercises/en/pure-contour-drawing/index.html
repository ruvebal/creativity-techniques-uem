Pure Contour Drawing

Draw the creases of your own palm for five minutes without looking at the paper. The drawing will not look like a hand; that is the point. It trains slow, exact looking and switches off the habit of drawing what you think things look like.

  • ~15 min
  • individual, then pairs to compare
  • technique pure-contour-drawing
  • unit U1
  • unit U4

Source book Edwards, Betty. 1999. The New Drawing on the Right Side of the Brain. New York: Jeremy P. Tarcher/Putnam.

Requirements

Time ~15 minutes
Group individual, then pairs to compare
Access Any complex object can replace the palm (crumpled paper, a leaf, a key) if holding the hand still is uncomfortable. Students may sit any way that lets them face away from the paper. A tablet with the screen covered works too. Low-vision students can trace a textured object by touch.

Materials

  • one sheet of paper, taped to the table
  • pencil or fine pen
  • timer

Before you start

  • No drawing skill needed; results are not judged on likeness.
  • Tape the paper down before you start so it does not move.
  • Keep this pass separate from project work; it is a perception warm-up.

Steps

Follow in order — your professor may shorten the clock.

  1. Tape the paper down. Hold the pencil on it, then turn your body so you face away from the paper.
  2. Set a timer for five minutes. Rest your other hand somewhere stable and look at the creases in your palm.
  3. Pick one crease. Move your eyes along it very slowly and let the pencil move at exactly the same speed.
  4. When the crease meets another line, follow the new line. Do not look at the paper until the timer ends.
  5. Look at the drawing. With a partner, name one detail you saw that you would normally never notice.
  6. Write one sentence on how your attention changed between minute one and minute five.

Source

Gaze at a single wrinkle in your palm. Place your pencil on the paper and begin to draw just that edge. As your eyes track the direction of the edge very slowly, one millimeter at a time, your pencil will record your perceptions. If the edge changes direction, so does your pencil. If the edge intersects with another edge, follow that new information slowly with your eyes, while your pencil simultaneously records every detail. An important point: Your pencil can record only what you see-nothing more, nothing less-at the moment of seeing. Your hand and pencil function like a seismograph, responding only to your actual perceptions.

— (Edwards 1999, 90)

Classroom note. Edwards demonstrates the method and gives it to drawing students as a single five-minute exercise. This classroom version keeps her five-minute timer, the palm subject, and the no-peeking rule; the partner comparison and the one-sentence attention note are professor additions that turn the drawing into evidence for the portfolio.

Portfolio

The contour drawing (photo or scan); one detail you noticed; one sentence on how attention changed.
