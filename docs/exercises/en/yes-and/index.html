Yes, And

Build one idea as a group by answering every offer with "Yes, and…". Then run the same round with "Yes, but…" and compare what happened to the idea and to the group.

  • ~25 min
  • 4–6 in a circle
  • technique improv-yes-and
  • unit U2
  • unit U4

Source book Nixon, Natalie. 2020. The Creativity Leap: Unleash Curiosity, Improvisation, and Intuition at Work. Oakland, CA: Berrett-Koehler.

Requirements

Time ~25 minutes
Group 4–6 in a circle
Access Offers can be spoken, written on cards passed around the circle, or typed into a shared chat. Students can sit or stand. A student who prefers not to speak may act as scribe and add in writing.

Materials

  • one opening prompt (for example "a library for people who hate reading")
  • a scribe with paper or a shared board
  • timer

Before you start

  • Listen to the whole offer before you add; you must build on the last thing said.
  • Everyone speaks at least once per round.
  • Keep the topic light for the first round so the rule, not the content, is the focus.

Steps

Follow in order — your professor may shorten the clock.

  1. Round one (eight minutes). The first person states the prompt as an idea. Each next person says "Yes, and…" and adds one detail that builds on the previous one.
  2. The scribe writes each addition as a chain.
  3. Round two (five minutes). Same prompt, but each person answers "Yes, but…" and names a problem before adding.
  4. Compare the two chains. Count the additions. Mark where the idea grew and where it stalled.
  5. Pick one surprising addition from round one and sketch it in two minutes.

Source

Being hyperpresent. One of the basic rules in improvisation is "Yes, and . . ." It is about agreement and building on a concept. It prevents the breakdown of momentum that comes from a "Yes, but . . ." or, even worse, straight-out "No" response. In order to build on the idea that someone has just offered to you, it is required that you engage in deep, intentional listening. It is an exercise in selflessness and generosity to give someone else your rapt attention and then amplify their idea, rather than wait for them to shut up so that you can focus on your own. Imagine if this principle were actively employed in our work environments. Think of the more energizing exchange of ideas we would have.

— (Nixon 2020, chap. 4)

Classroom note. Nixon presents "Yes, and" as a basic rule of improvisation and argues for its use at work. The two-round comparison, timings, scribe role, and closing sketch are professor adaptations. The "Yes, but" round is a contrast, not a recommendation; critique returns later in a separate selection step.

Portfolio

Both chains (photo or text); addition counts; one sentence on where the idea stalled; the two-minute sketch.
