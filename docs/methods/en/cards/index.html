Method cards

One card per technique or practice method: when to use it, how long it takes and the steps. Print this page (one card never splits across pages) or keep it open in class. The steps are written for this course; where a card names a source, the full entry is in the list at the end.

Practice method

Project-based learning

When to use: Learn by delivering a complete brief under constraints — not by collecting named techniques in isolation. The project is the unit of meaning; techniques serve the brief.

Time: Not fixed: a cycle you repeat across a project.

  1. Receive or co-frame a design brief with criteria and deadline.
  2. Plan milestones (research, diverge, select, develop, deliver).
  3. Submit artefact + process note that explains decisions.

Source: Classroom adaptation.

Practice method

Problem-solving studio

When to use: Treat creativity as situated problem work — frame the issue, explore options, test fitness, and defend the choice. Wicked briefs need framing before tools.

Time: Not fixed: a cycle you repeat across a project.

  1. Name the problem and what would count as a good-enough answer.
  2. Generate options without early judgment.
  3. Select with explicit criteria and test against the brief.

Source: Classroom adaptation.

Practice method

Diverge–converge workshop

When to use: Time-boxed sessions that separate opening (many options) from closing (selection). Fluency without flexibility is one idea in many costumes.

Time: Not fixed: a cycle you repeat across a project.

  1. Protect a diverge window (quantity + variety).
  2. Switch modes deliberately into converge (criteria + pick).
  3. Record what was discarded and why.

Source: Classroom adaptation.

Practice method

Critique cycle

When to use: Peer and tutor critique as a learning method — give/receive feedback that improves the next iteration, not vanity praise. Process over polish alone.

Time: Not fixed: a cycle you repeat across a project.

  1. Present the brief, constraints, and current state briefly.
  2. Ask for specific feedback (criteria, gaps, next test).
  3. Log one change you will make before the next checkpoint.

Source: Classroom adaptation.

Practice method

Iterative prototyping

When to use: Make rough, early versions so reaction teaches faster than speculation. Defer final polish until the idea has survived contact with users or peers.

Time: Not fixed: a cycle you repeat across a project.

  1. Prototype the riskiest assumption first.
  2. Show it to someone who can react honestly.
  3. Revise once; decide when the brief is answered.

Source: Classroom adaptation.

Practice method

Process portfolio

When to use: Evidence of learning is the trail — sketches, discarded options, criteria, and reflections — not only the final image. Authorship includes how meaning was put into circulation and revised.

Time: Not fixed: a cycle you repeat across a project.

  1. Keep a dated process folder for every project.
  2. Include at least one discarded path with a one-line reason.
  3. Disclose tools (including AI) and what you still own as judgment.

Source: Classroom adaptation.

Technique

Alternative Uses Task (AUT)

When to use: When you need many options; opening (divergent). Course units: U1, U2.

Time: About 15 minutes. · Group: individual, class pooling

  1. Show one everyday object (brick, paperclip, shoe) and start a visible timer.
  2. Each student lists as many different uses as they can, alone and in silence.
  3. Stop; each student counts ideas (fluency) and use categories (flexibility).
  4. Pool the class lists; a use that appears on only one list counts as original.
  5. Each student picks one use and adds detail to it (elaboration).
  6. Discuss which category shifts produced the less obvious uses.

Source: (Chen 2011, 26). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Brainstorming (Osborn ground rules)

When to use: When you need many options; opening (divergent). Course units: U2, U4.

Time: About 20 minutes. · Group: 4–8

  1. State one problem as a question everyone can read.
  2. Agree the ground rule: no criticism until the idea round ends.
  3. A scribe records every idea, numbered, in the speakers' words.
  4. Invite combinations and improvements of ideas already on the board.
  5. Stop at the time limit, not when ideas slow down.
  6. Only then move to a separate selection technique.

Source: (Osborn 1942, chap. 4). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Analogy transfer

When to use: When you need many options; opening (divergent). Course units: U2, U3.

Time: About 20 minutes. · Group: 1–4

  1. Describe the problem in one plain sentence.
  2. Choose an analogy that has its own clear line of development (a process, a story, a system).
  3. Develop the analogy on its own terms for a few minutes.
  4. Carry each development back to the problem and note what it suggests.
  5. Keep the suggestions that change how the problem looks.

Source: (de Bono 1970, chap. “Analogies”). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Random word stimulation

When to use: When you need many options; opening (divergent). Course units: U2, U5.

Time: About 10 minutes. · Group: 1–4

  1. Write the problem at the top of the page.
  2. Draw a random noun (dictionary page, word list or card deck).
  3. List features and associations of the word, without looking at the problem.
  4. Connect each association back to the problem and write the idea it triggers.
  5. Stay with this one word for a single fixed slot of 3 to 5 minutes; do not switch words when it feels stuck.
  6. Use a different word on another day; in class, compare ideas with classmates who drew other words.

Source: (de Bono 1970, chap. “Random stimulation”). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

PO provocation

When to use: When you need many options; opening (divergent). Course units: U2, U3.

Time: About 15 minutes. · Group: 1–5

  1. State the usual assumption about the situation.
  2. Write a deliberately impossible or reversed statement and mark it PO.
  3. Do not judge the statement; ask what it would lead to (movement).
  4. Note every practical idea that the movement produced.
  5. Drop the provocation and keep the ideas.

Source: (de Bono 1970, chap. “The new word PO”). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Reversal method

When to use: When you need many options; opening (divergent). Course units: U2.

Time: About 15 minutes. · Group: 1–4

  1. Write the situation as it is normally described.
  2. Reverse the direction, roles or relationship (who serves whom, what moves what).
  3. Describe the reversed situation in a few lines.
  4. Note any arrangement the reversal makes visible.
  5. Turn one of them into a usable idea.

Source: (de Bono 1970, chap. “The reversal method”). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Quota of alternatives

When to use: When you need many options; opening (divergent). Course units: U1, U2.

Time: About 10 minutes. · Group: 1–4

  1. Choose a situation, image or design that already has an obvious reading.
  2. Set a fixed number of alternative readings or approaches (for example five).
  3. Keep going until the quota is met, even when the first answer seems right.
  4. Compare the first and the last alternatives.

Source: (de Bono 1970, chap. “The generation of alternatives”). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Fractionation

When to use: When you need many options; opening (divergent). Course units: U2, U3.

Time: About 20 minutes. · Group: 1–3

  1. Split the problem into two or three parts in the usual way.
  2. Split it again in a different way, ignoring the first split.
  3. Keep splitting until the parts are small and unusual.
  4. Recombine parts across the different splits into new arrangements.
  5. Keep one arrangement that suggests a new approach.

Source: (de Bono 1970, chap. “Fractionation”). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Crazy 8s

When to use: When you need many options; opening (divergent). Course units: U2, U3.

Time: About 10 minutes. · Group: individual

  1. Take your strongest idea so far.
  2. Fold a sheet into eight panels.
  3. Sketch one variation per panel, one minute each, against a timer.
  4. Keep sketches rough; words are allowed.
  5. Circle the variation you would develop and say why.

Source: (Knapp, Zeratsky, and Kowitz 2016, chap. 9). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

What-if (counterfactual) prompts

When to use: When you need many options; opening (divergent). Course units: U2, U4.

Time: About 15 minutes. · Group: 1–4

  1. Describe the current situation or design in two lines.
  2. Write five "what if ... had been different?" questions about it.
  3. Answer each with one consequence.
  4. Turn the most useful consequence into an alternative idea.

Source: (Wong, Galinsky, and Kray 2009, 161, 168). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Seed collection

When to use: When you need many options; opening (divergent). Course units: U2, U6.

Time: About 30 minutes. · Group: individual

  1. For one week, note small starting points (a phrase, a sound, an image) in one place.
  2. Do not compare or rank them while collecting.
  3. At the end of the week, pick three seeds that still interest you.
  4. Develop each in a different direction for ten minutes.
  5. Keep the seed that grew most; note why.

Source: (Rubin 2023, chap. “Seeds”). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Temporary rules (constraint-based making)

When to use: When you need many options; opening (divergent). Course units: U5, U6.

Time: About 25 minutes. · Group: 1–2

  1. Choose a short piece to make (a text, a layout, a sound loop).
  2. Draw or set one strict formal rule (no letter e, only five shapes, one colour).
  3. Make the piece under the rule within the time limit.
  4. Make a second version without the rule.
  5. Compare which version is more surprising and why.

Source: (Rubin 2023, chap. “Temporary Rules”). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Lightning demos

When to use: When you need many options; opening (divergent). Course units: U3, U4, U5.

Time: About 30 minutes. · Group: 4–8

  1. Each student brings two or three existing solutions from other fields or products.
  2. Each gives a three-minute tour of what is good about one example.
  3. A scribe draws a quick sketch and headline of each big idea on the board.
  4. The group looks for ideas to borrow and remix, not to copy.

Source: (Knapp, Zeratsky, and Kowitz 2016, chap. 8). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

How Might We notes

When to use: When the problem is still unclear and you want to frame or reframe it; opening and closing. Course units: U1, U3, U4.

Time: About 25 minutes. · Group: 3–8

  1. Listen to a short briefing or interview about the problem.
  2. Each student writes one "How might we ...?" question per sticky note.
  3. Put all notes on a wall and group similar ones.
  4. Each student votes for the questions worth working on.
  5. Choose one question as the brief for the next technique.

Source: (Knapp, Zeratsky, and Kowitz 2016, chap. 6). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Challenging assumptions (Why technique)

When to use: When the problem is still unclear and you want to frame or reframe it; opening (divergent). Course units: U1, U3.

Time: About 15 minutes. · Group: 2–4

  1. List what everyone assumes about the situation.
  2. Ask "why is it done this way?" about each assumption.
  3. Keep asking why about each answer, two or three times.
  4. Mark answers that rest on habit rather than need.
  5. Rewrite the brief without one habit-based assumption.

Source: (de Bono 1970, chap. “Challenging assumptions”). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Entry point and attention area

When to use: When the problem is still unclear and you want to frame or reframe it; opening (divergent). Course units: U3.

Time: About 20 minutes. · Group: 1–4

  1. Write the problem and list its parts.
  2. Note where you would normally start.
  3. Start instead from a different part (the end, the user, the material).
  4. Shift attention to a part you would usually ignore.
  5. Compare the paths each entry point opened.

Source: (de Bono 1970, chap. “Choice of entry point and attention area”). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Dominant idea and crucial factor

When to use: When the problem is still unclear and you want to frame or reframe it; closing (convergent). Course units: U3, U4.

Time: About 15 minutes. · Group: 1–4

  1. Read a brief or an existing design.
  2. Write the idea that dominates how everyone is approaching it.
  3. Name the factor treated as crucial (the one nobody would drop).
  4. Ask what happens if that factor is relaxed.
  5. Record one approach that escapes the dominant idea.

Source: (de Bono 1970, chap. “Dominant ideas and crucial factors”). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Five whys

When to use: When the problem is still unclear and you want to frame or reframe it; closing (convergent). Course units: U3, U4.

Time: About 15 minutes. · Group: 2–4

  1. State the observed problem or failure.
  2. Ask why it happened; write the answer.
  3. Ask why again of that answer, up to five times.
  4. Stop when an answer points to something you can change in the design.
  5. Check that the chain does not end at blaming a person.

Source: (Norman 2013, chap. 5). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Problem finding (presented vs discovered problems)

When to use: When the problem is still unclear and you want to frame or reframe it; opening (divergent). Course units: U1, U6.

Time: About 25 minutes. · Group: 1–3

  1. Take the brief as given and write it down.
  2. Observe the situation for ten minutes and list problems the brief does not mention.
  3. Rewrite one discovered problem as a new brief.
  4. Compare the given and the discovered brief with a peer.

Source: (Csikszentmihalyi 1996, chap. 4). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Problem–solution reframing (wicked problems)

When to use: When the problem is still unclear and you want to frame or reframe it; opening and closing. Course units: U1, U3.

Time: About 20 minutes. · Group: 2–4

  1. Write three different formulations of the same problem.
  2. For each formulation, sketch the solution it implies.
  3. Note how each solution changes the problem you see.
  4. Choose the formulation you will work with and say what it excludes.

Source: (Buchanan 1992, 16). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Double-diamond plan

When to use: When the problem is still unclear and you want to frame or reframe it; opening and closing. Course units: U3, U4.

Time: About 20 minutes. · Group: 3–6

  1. Draw two diamonds side by side - problem space, then solution space.
  2. Plan an opening (diverge) and a closing (converge) move for each diamond.
  3. Assign one technique from this catalogue to each of the four moves.
  4. Mark where the team will check whether it is solving the right problem.

Source: (Norman 2013, chap. 6). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Contextual observation

When to use: When the problem is still unclear and you want to frame or reframe it; opening (divergent). Course units: U3, U4.

Time: About 30 minutes. · Group: 1–3

  1. Choose a place where people do the activity you are designing for.
  2. Watch for twenty minutes; note what people do, not what you think they want.
  3. Mark workarounds and moments of hesitation.
  4. Write three questions the observation raised.

Source: (Norman 2013, chap. 6). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Hits and dot voting

When to use: When you must choose among ideas with a stated criterion; closing (convergent). Course units: U2, U4.

Time: About 15 minutes. · Group: 3–10

  1. Put all ideas on the wall, one per note or sheet.
  2. Read in silence; each student places small dots on parts that look strong (hits).
  3. Each student then gets one larger vote for one idea.
  4. Cluster the dots and read out the top three.
  5. The decider (or the group) chooses, and records why.

Source: (Knapp, Zeratsky, and Kowitz 2016, chap. 10). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Note-and-vote

When to use: When you must choose among ideas with a stated criterion; closing (convergent). Course units: U3, U4.

Time: About 15 minutes. · Group: 3–8

  1. Each student writes their best ideas on paper, in silence.
  2. Each picks their single favourite.
  3. Go round the group; each reads out the favourite and it is written on the board.
  4. Everyone votes once, in silence.
  5. The decider makes the final call.

Source: (Knapp, Zeratsky, and Kowitz 2016, chap. 11). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

PMI (Plus, Minus, Interesting)

When to use: When you must choose among ideas with a stated criterion; closing (convergent). Course units: U2, U4.

Time: About 10 minutes. · Group: 1–4

  1. Take one idea at a time.
  2. List its plus points for two minutes.
  3. List its minus points for two minutes.
  4. List what is interesting about it, whatever the verdict.
  5. Decide - keep, change or drop - and note the reason.

Source: (de Bono 1985, 12–13). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Six Thinking Hats

When to use: When you must choose among ideas with a stated criterion; opening and closing. Course units: U2, U4.

Time: About 30 minutes. · Group: 3–8

  1. Name the decision or idea to examine.
  2. Agree a sequence of hats for the session (for example white, green, yellow, black, red, blue).
  3. Everyone thinks in the same hat at the same time for a fixed slot.
  4. Record contributions under the current hat only.
  5. Close with the blue hat - summary and next step.

Source: (de Bono 1985). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Delay-judgement checkpoint

When to use: When you develop and test one chosen idea; opening and closing. Course units: U3.

Time: About 20 minutes. · Group: 2–5

  1. Agree a generation phase with no evaluation.
  2. Set a checkpoint time when evaluation opens.
  3. At the checkpoint, apply one stated criterion only.
  4. Agree an exit rule - what result ends the iteration.

Source: (Osborn 1942, chap. 4). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Parallel prototyping

When to use: When you develop and test one chosen idea; opening and closing. Course units: U3, U5.

Time: About 45 minutes. · Group: 1–3

  1. Make three different quick prototypes of the same brief before getting feedback.
  2. Get feedback on all three at once.
  3. Note what each prototype taught you.
  4. Combine the strongest elements into the next version.
  5. Compare with what one prototype at a time would have shown.

Source: (Dow et al. 2010, 18:1). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Storyboarding

When to use: When you develop and test one chosen idea; closing (convergent). Course units: U3, U4.

Time: About 40 minutes. · Group: 1–5

  1. Draw a grid of eight to fifteen frames.
  2. Choose an opening scene - where the user first meets the idea.
  3. Fill the frames step by step with rough sketches and short captions.
  4. Use the strongest parts of earlier sketches rather than new ideas.
  5. Walk a peer through the storyboard and note where they hesitate.

Source: (Knapp, Zeratsky, and Kowitz 2016, chap. 12). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Question-led rough prototype

When to use: When you develop and test one chosen idea; closing (convergent). Course units: U3, U5.

Time: About 45 minutes. · Group: 1–4

  1. Write the one question this prototype must answer.
  2. Decide whether it tests role, look and feel, or how it works (course wording).
  3. Build only the surface needed to answer it.
  4. Test with one person and record the answer to the question.

Source: (Knapp, Zeratsky, and Kowitz 2016, chap. 13). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Seeing-as sketch cycles

When to use: When you develop and test one chosen idea; opening and closing. Course units: U3.

Time: About 25 minutes. · Group: individual

  1. Sketch a first idea quickly.
  2. Look at the sketch and write what it could also be (seeing as).
  3. Sketch again from that new reading.
  4. Note what you now know about the problem (seeing that).
  5. Repeat for three cycles.

Source: (Cross 2006, 86). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Five-user test interviews

When to use: When you develop and test one chosen idea; closing (convergent). Course units: U3, U4.

Time: About 60 minutes. · Group: 3–5 plus five testers

  1. Recruit five people close to the intended users.
  2. Each tests the prototype one at a time while the team watches.
  3. The interviewer asks open questions and lets the person think aloud.
  4. Observers note reactions on a shared grid.
  5. Look for patterns seen with three or more people.

Source: (Knapp, Zeratsky, and Kowitz 2016, chap. 15). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Reflection-in-action log

When to use: When you want to record and learn from your own process; opening and closing. Course units: U3, U6.

Time: About 10 minutes. · Group: individual

  1. While working, stop when something surprises you.
  2. Note what you expected and what happened.
  3. Write the new move you will try because of it.
  4. After the session, link the moves into a short account of the conversation with the material.

Source: (Schön 1983, 68, 79). Steps: classroom adaptation.

Technique

Open-monitoring warm-up

When to use: As an optional start that uses movement or attention; opening (divergent). Course units: U2, U6.

Time: About 3 minutes. · Group: whole class

  1. Sit comfortably; eyes open or closed as preferred.
  2. For three minutes, notice whatever thoughts, sounds or sensations come, without holding on to any.
  3. Gently return to open noticing when you get caught in one thought.
  4. Move straight into a divergent task.

Optional: you may opt out and sit quietly or start the next task instead.

Source: (Colzato, Ozturk, and Hommel 2012, 1). Steps: classroom adaptation.
