Lesson: Who Designs a Product—and Who Owns Its
Story?
Core question for students: Is design the shape of an object, the system that makes it usable,
the patent that protects it, or the economic machine that distributes it? This session uses
Edison-era products to examine creativity, appropriation, and the hidden social infrastructure
behind familiar objects.

Learning objectives
By the end, students should be able to:
    Distinguish invention, innovation, design, appropriation, and commercialization.
    Analyze products as assemblages of form, function, manufacture, law, capital, and cultural
    meaning.
    Critically assess how “authorship” is constructed around technologies.
    Apply these questions to their own design practice: interior, industrial, graphic, product, or
    speculative design.

Framing: design beyond the object
For designers, a product is never only an artifact. A light bulb is glass, tungsten, socket
geometry, electrical infrastructure, pricing, labor, energy policy, and a cultural metaphor for
“ideas.” A projector is optics, film format, exhibition space, ticket sales, censorship, and
collective spectatorship.
This lesson therefore treats design at three levels:

          Level                              Question                                             Example

    Artifact            What is it made of, and how does it work?               Filament, glass envelope, screw base

    System              What infrastructure makes it function?                  Generators, wiring, meters, utilities

    Political economy   Who profits, controls access, and defines authorship?   Patent holders, banks, utilities, distributors


Case 1: The incandescent lamp
The common claim that Edison “invented the light bulb” is historically incomplete. Edison’s
laboratory developed a commercially practical incandescent lighting system, but earlier
inventors had worked on incandescent lamps, and the U.S. Supreme Court later invalidated a
patent belonging to William Sawyer and Albon Man; the Court did not decide that Edison
invented the light bulb. [1]
From a design perspective, Edison’s major contribution was system design: a durable lamp,
generators, distribution wiring, meters, sockets, and a business model that could make electric
lighting viable. This is crucial for product and industrial designers: the breakthrough was not
only the object, but the interoperable ecosystem around it.
Discussion prompt: If you redesign a lamp today, are you designing a light source, a domestic
ritual, an energy interface, or a data-connected service?

Case 2: The phonograph
Edison’s phonograph is a stronger case of genuine invention, but even here authorship was
collective. Edison sketched the concept after work on telephone technology; machinist John
Kruesi built the first tinfoil machine from Edison’s drawings. Edison filed on December 24,
1877, and received the patent on February 19, 1878. [2] [3]
French inventor Charles Cros had earlier proposed a similar recording principle in April 1877,
though he did not build a working device. Léon Scott’s 1857 phonautograph also anticipated
aspects of sound recording. [4] [2]
Design lesson: The phonograph shows how a concept may circulate before one actor turns it
into a working, manufacturable product. Creativity is often a relay, not a single act.
Studio exercise: Redesign the phonograph for a contemporary social need—memory
preservation, oral history, accessibility, or community archiving—while identifying who would
own the data, hardware, and distribution.

Case 3: The Kinetoscope and cinema projector
The motion-picture case is especially useful because it separates invention from the eventual
medium. Edison commissioned William Kennedy Laurie Dickson to develop a motion-picture
apparatus; Dickson performed much of the experimentation. The result was the Kinetograph
camera and Kinetoscope, a coin-operated peephole device for one viewer at a time. [5] [6] [7] [8]
The public cinema projector was not Edison’s original design goal. After the Lumière brothers
demonstrated their Cinématographe in Paris in 1895—a device that could film, print, and
project—Edison’s organization added projection to reach larger audiences. [9] [10] [5]
This is a powerful design contrast:
    Kinetoscope: private, boxed, individual, coin-operated.
    Cinématographe: public, projected, social, portable.
The difference is not merely technical. It changes the social form of the experience: from a
solitary curiosity to collective cinema, from a machine in an arcade to an institution that
shapes public culture.
Design prompt: Choose a contemporary screen technology—VR headset, smartphone,
interactive installation—and redesign it by shifting its social scale: private to public, personal to
communal, owned to shared.

Case 4: Radio and the myth of single authorship
Radio is essential for correcting the Edison-centered narrative. Edison was not the principal
inventor of radio. Radio emerged through the work of figures including James Clerk Maxwell,
Heinrich Hertz, Oliver Lodge, Nikola Tesla, Guglielmo Marconi, and others. Marconi is often
publicly remembered as “the inventor of radio,” while Tesla, Lodge, and many contributors
were marginalized in popular accounts.
For design students, radio demonstrates that a product category can be socially attributed to
one name even when it results from distributed scientific and engineering work. The “radio
set” as a domestic object was also a design act: it translated invisible electromagnetic
communication into a cabinet, dial, speaker, living-room ritual, national broadcast culture, and
advertising economy.
Critical question: When a company owns the brand, patent, spectrum license, transmitter
network, and retail channel, who is the real author of the user experience?

Case 5: Edison’s patent system and the “Edison Trust”
The Motion Picture Patents Company, or “Edison Trust,” is the clearest example of design
power becoming market power. Edison and allied patent holders pooled patents over cameras,
projectors, and film stock, then controlled licensing across production, distribution, and
exhibition. [11] [12] [13]
The trust attempted to make independent filmmakers dependent on licensed equipment and
film stock. Independent producers faced extensive litigation; the system was dissolved under
U.S. antitrust law in 1915, with the court rejecting the use of patents as a weapon to disable
competitors. [14]
For designers, this raises an uncomfortable point: the same standardized film format that
enabled industrial efficiency also enabled control. Standardization can democratize production
by making tools compatible, but it can also create chokepoints when one actor owns the
standard.

Banking, capital, and the designer’s blind spot
Edison’s enterprises were not financed only by sales. J.P. Morgan and Anthony Drexel helped
finance and consolidate Edison’s electrical businesses; Edison General Electric was formed in
1889, then merged with Thomson-Houston in 1892 to create General Electric. [15] [16] [17]
The result was paradoxical: Edison lost operational control of the company that carried part of
his name. Thomson-Houston management led the new General Electric, while financiers
shaped the corporate structure. [16] [17]
This is the “lower-level” design discussion students need: every product exists inside a financial
architecture. A chair is affected by material suppliers and retail margins; a medical device by
insurance systems; a lamp by utility regulation; an app by platform fees and venture capital.
Key idea: Banking does not merely fund design; it can determine which designs survive, who
receives credit, and which futures become economically visible.

The ethical issue is not that Edison patented inventions—patents can protect genuine creative
labor. The issue is when patents, branding, litigation, and finance convert one actor’s
contribution into control over an entire field.

Creative techniques for the studio

1. Product biography
Trace one object from raw material to disposal. Include designers, factory workers, patent
holders, investors, distributors, users, and waste streams.

2. Authorship map
Draw a network diagram: inventors, assistants, funders, manufacturers, regulators, and users.
Mark where public history gives credit and where it disappears.

3. Counter-design
Redesign the product under a different ownership model: open source, cooperative, public
utility, repairable commons, or community-owned infrastructure.
4. Format politics
Take a format—35 mm film, MP3, PDF, USB-C, a building typology—and ask: What does it make
easy? What does it make impossible? Who benefits from its standardization?

5. Speculative patent
Write a patent-style document for an everyday social practice: “a method for sharing tools,” “a
system for collective memory,” “a device for slowing attention.” Then ask what happens when it
is owned by a bank, a platform, or a public institution.

Assignment
Brief: Select one product—lamp, phonograph, Kinetoscope, cinema projector, radio, telephone,
or a contemporary equivalent. Produce:
 1. A redesigned artifact or system.
 2. An authorship and value-flow map.
 3. A one-page critical analysis of ownership, access, labor, and finance.
 4. A prototype or visual proposal showing an alternative economic model.
The goal is not to condemn innovation, but to make designers aware that every object carries a
political economy. Design is not only making things desirable; it is deciding who may
participate in their creation, use, repair, and benefit. [5] [11] [14] [15]
